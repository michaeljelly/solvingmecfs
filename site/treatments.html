Treatments people are trying

No drug is approved for ME/CFS, and many patients try treatments that are plausible but unproven. This page lists 61 of them. Each entry says which step of the working model it acts on and why the model predicts it could help, then grades the evidence that it does, the risks and the cost.

Pacing and symptom management

Keeping activity below the level that provokes post-exertional malaise. Patients rate it the most helpful measure, but no trial has tested heart-rate-guided pacing.

Pacing with heart-rate limitsReasonable to try Rated most helpful of over 150 treatments by ~3,925 patients (net ~75%, Eckey 2025 survey); in the first positive randomised trial, 12 weeks of coached structured pacing cut how often and how badly PEM happened vs usual care in 298 people with long COVID (PEM frequency -0.46, 95% CI -0.65 to -0.27; RECOVER-ENERGIZE, posted results, unblinded); PACE's adaptive pacing arm (n=160) was no better than usual care, but neither trial tested heart-rate-guided envelope pacing. Positive RCTSteps 567Patients +75% net · n=803
Why it might help, in the model

If crashes are steps 5 to 7 firing when demand outruns delivery, keeping demand below the threshold should prevent them. Pacing treats nothing; it stops the loop being driven harder.

What it does

Keeps effort below the level that triggers post-exertional malaise, using a heart-rate alarm (often set around resting rate plus 15 bpm, or 55-60% of age-predicted maximum) and planned rest. In the model it stops steps 5 to 7 from firing, rather than treating anything.

Who it might suit

Everyone with post-exertional malaise. Most useful for people who crash after delays they cannot predict, and for carers who need an objective stop signal.

Risks

Few. Heart-rate limits miss cognitive, sensory and orthostatic load, and beta-blockers or ivabradine make the numbers unreliable. Strict tracking can add anxiety.

Severe patients

For bedbound patients, sitting up, talking, light and noise count as exertion; overnight resting heart rate and HRV trends are often more useful than daytime alarms.

Lying-down and upright time managementReasonable to try No treatment trial; tilt studies show cardiac output and brain blood flow fall in most patients when upright (82% abnormal brain flow despite normal heart rate in van Campen 2020), and lying down removes that load. Mechanism onlySteps 35
Why it might help, in the model

Standing is the commonest everyday demand on step 5, because low volume (step 3) leaves the brain under-supplied when upright. Less upright time means less under-perfusion.

What it does

Counts upright minutes as part of the energy budget: sitting with legs raised, lying for tasks that can be done flat, and short upright spells broken by lying down. It reduces the time the brain and muscles are under-supplied.

Who it might suit

Anyone with orthostatic intolerance (light-headedness, brain fog or heart racing on standing), whether or not POTS criteria are met.

Risks

Very low. Long-term complete bed rest has its own costs (muscle and bone loss, clots), so keep whatever gentle movement is tolerated lying down.

Severe patients

Usually already in place by necessity; a reclining wheelchair or bed that raises the head slightly at night (some POTS clinics advise 10-15 degrees) may help.

Access and cost

OTC

Sources van Campen 2020, Clin Neurophysiol Pract (cerebral blood flow on tilt) unverified; van Campen 2021, Medicina (compression, randomised crossover)

Circulation and blood volume

Many patients have low blood volume and too little blood returning to the heart when upright, whether or not they meet POTS criteria. These measures support volume or venous return; the cheap ones are rated highly by patients, and the drug trials are small or negative.

Oral fluids, electrolytes and saltReasonable to try No ME/CFS trial; fluids and electrolytes are the second-highest-rated treatment in the Eckey 2025 survey (net ~69%), and in POTS oral rehydration improved orthostatic tolerance in a small comparison with IV saline (Medow 2019). Case series or surveysSteps 35Patients +68% net · n=2860
Why it might help, in the model

Directly targets step 3. If low plasma volume limits filling of the heart, more fluid and sodium should raise stroke volume and brain blood flow when upright. It does not correct red-cell volume.

What it does

Expands plasma volume so more blood returns to the heart when upright. Typical POTS advice is 2-3 litres of fluid and 6-10 g of salt a day (3-5 g sodium), spread through the day, or oral rehydration solution.

Who it might suit

People with orthostatic intolerance, low blood pressure, dark urine or high plasma osmolality, and those who pass a lot of dilute urine.

Risks

Not for people with high blood pressure, heart failure or kidney disease. Salt tablets can cause nausea; take with food. Very large water intake without salt can lower blood sodium.

Severe patients

Swallowing and toileting are effortful in severe ME; electrolyte solutions in small, frequent sips and a bedside urinal bottle help.

Compression garmentsReasonable to try Randomised crossover in 16 ME/CFS patients on tilt: compression stockings cut the fall in cardiac output from 27% to 15% and in brain blood flow from 25% to 14% (van Campen 2021); this is an acute measurement, not a symptom trial. Positive RCTSteps 35Patients +62% net · n=952
Why it might help, in the model

Reduces pooling in leg and gut veins, which raises the return of blood to the heart (step 3) and so brain and muscle perfusion (step 5). Compression halved the fall in cardiac output on tilt in one study.

What it does

Squeezes leg and abdominal veins so less blood pools below the heart when upright. Abdominal binders matter as much as stockings, because much of the pooling is in the gut.

Who it might suit

Anyone with orthostatic symptoms; third in the patient survey ratings (net ~62%), especially in the POTS-dominant cluster.

Risks

Heat, itching and the effort of putting them on. Thigh-high or waist-high class 1-2 (20-30 mmHg) is typical; knee-high adds little. Not for people with peripheral arterial disease.

Severe patients

Only useful when upright; putting on stockings can itself cause a crash, so a carer's help or an easy-on abdominal binder may be better.

MidodrineWith a doctor No ME/CFS outcome trial; small acute crossover studies in POTS and orthostatic intolerance show lower standing heart rate, and phenylephrine (a related drug) restored brain blood flow and working memory on tilt in ME/CFS (Medow & Stewart 2024). Mechanism onlySteps 35Patients +30% net · n=174
Why it might help, in the model

Constricts veins and arteries, partly compensating for low volume and poor venous tone (step 3) and reducing the fall in brain blood flow when upright (step 5).

What it does

Tightens arteries and veins for about 4 hours per dose, raising blood pressure and reducing pooling when upright.

Who it might suit

People whose problem is low blood pressure or pooling on standing, who are upright for set periods during the day.

Risks

High blood pressure when lying down (the main danger), goosebumps, scalp tingling, urinary retention. Not for heart disease, kidney failure or uncontrolled hypertension. Last dose at least 4 hours before lying down for the night.

Severe patients

Usually unsuitable for bedbound patients because of lying-down hypertension.

PyridostigmineWith a doctor A single 60 mg dose raised peak oxygen uptake vs placebo on a repeat invasive exercise test in 45 ME/CFS patients with preload failure, largely because the placebo group got worse (Joseph 2022); a 6-10-week long-COVID RCT (n=32, conference abstract) was negative overall. Why it might help, in the model

Strengthens the autonomic signal that tightens veins, addressing preload failure (step 3) and, through it, under-perfusion during exertion (step 5). This fits the subgroup with low filling pressure on invasive testing best.

What it does

Boosts acetylcholine signalling in autonomic nerves, which helps tighten veins and return blood to the heart (preload).

Who it might suit

POTS or suspected preload failure; possibly those with small-fibre neuropathy on skin biopsy.

Risks

Gut cramps, diarrhoea and urgency (about one in five stop for this in POTS series), sweating, increased saliva, muscle twitching. Caution in asthma and slow heart rate.

Severe patients

Bowel urgency is a real burden when getting to the toilet is hard; start at 30 mg or lower.

What it does

Severe patients

IV saline infusionsSpecialist Uncontrolled Swedish series of 22 ME/CFS patients given ~1.6 L every third week for 9 weeks: symptoms, POTS scores and quality of life improved, with effects wearing off between infusions (Sjögren 2025); no randomised trial has reported. Case series or surveysSteps 35Patients +70% net · n=193
Why it might help, in the model

Corrects step 3 quickly and fully for a few days. A reliable short-term response is evidence that volume matters for that patient; its brevity suggests volume is not the whole story.

What it does

Puts volume straight into the circulation, temporarily correcting low blood volume and preload. The benefit usually lasts days, not weeks.

Who it might suit

Patients with clear hypovolaemia or POTS who still struggle after oral fluids, salt and compression; some use it before a necessary outing or procedure.

Risks

Cannula infection, vein damage, fluid overload in heart or kidney disease. Regular infusions often lead to a port or PICC line, which carries a real risk of line infection and clots; avoid long-term lines for this alone.

Severe patients

Home infusion services avoid travel; the trip to a clinic can cost more than the infusion gives.

What it does

Slows the heart's pacemaker without lowering blood pressure, so the heart fills for longer between beats.

Risks

Sleep and arousal

Treatments aimed at sympathetic arousal and disturbed sleep (steps 7 and 8). The evidence is one retrospective series, small pilots, and one well-run trial that showed harm.

Low-dose aripiprazoleWith a doctor Retrospective review at one Stanford clinic: about 74% of 101 patients on 0.25-2 mg reported improvement in brain fog, fatigue or sleep; no control group and no randomised trial yet (Crosby 2021). Case series or surveysSteps 7Patients +30% net · n=136
Why it might help, in the model

Targets the brain end of the sickness response (step 7): interferon studies show part of it runs through reduced dopamine signalling in the basal ganglia, and reward-related basal ganglia activity is lower in ME/CFS.

What it does

At very low doses it nudges dopamine signalling and may calm microglia, targeting the basal-ganglia dopamine dip in the sickness response.

Who it might suit

Prominent brain fog and unrefreshing sleep, once sleep is reasonably steady.

Risks

Insomnia, restlessness (akathisia), vivid dreams; rarely movement disorders or impulse-control problems (gambling, shopping). Needs a liquid formulation or tablet splitting to reach 0.25-1 mg. The ME Association advised against use outside trials in 2021.

Severe patients

Start at 0.25 mg or lower; restlessness is hard to bear when bedbound.

Graded exercise therapy (GET)Avoid NICE 2021 says do not offer GET or any programme of fixed incremental increases in activity; GET was the most harmful-rated treatment in the ~3,925-person survey (net about -72%), PACE's modest self-reported gains did not show on objective measures or persist, guided self-help GET gave a self-rated fatigue gain at 12 weeks (GETSET, n=211), and in children GET was no better than activity management (MAGENTA, n=241). Negative RCTSteps 567Patients -72% net · n=299
Why it might help, in the model

Fixed increases in exertion repeatedly drive steps 5 to 7, the sequence the model holds responsible for the crash, so the model predicts harm in people with post-exertional malaise.

What it does

Fixed, stepwise increases in exercise, based on the now largely refuted theory that deconditioning and fear of activity maintain the illness.

Who it might suit

No one with post-exertional malaise. Gentle, symptom-led movement within the energy envelope is a different thing.

Risks

Worsening that can be prolonged or permanent, including moving from moderate to severe. A 2026 Charité rehab study left 42 of 97 worse even with pacing elements.

Severe patients

Severe patients have the most to lose; refuse any programme that sets activity targets regardless of symptoms.

specialist
