A working model of ME/CFS

My best current understanding of how the published findings fit together: eight steps, how strongly each is supported, how they may cause one another, and the result that would show each one wrong.

By Michael Jelly · Updated {{built}} · Evidence for every step is graded in the atlas

What this is

This is an attempt to explain the findings that have held up in ME/CFS research with as few steps as possible, and to say plainly where it fails. Each step is an abnormality, graded by how well it has been shown in patients. The arrows between steps are a hypothesis about cause and effect, and most have not been tested. Every step names a result that would disprove it, and the open questions and proposed experiments are chosen to test the weakest parts first. The model will change as results come in.

The closest published model is Wirth and Scheibenbogen's (2020, 2021): autoantibodies impair β2-adrenergic signalling, muscle is under-perfused during exertion, and sodium and then calcium overload damage mitochondria and the endothelium. Steps 4 and 5 here are close to their core, and step 3 draws on Systrom's invasive exercise work on preload failure and van Campen's measurements of brain blood flow on tilt. This model differs in not depending on receptor autoantibodies, which have not held up in controlled trials, and in placing inherited risk in the brain's control of circulation, which is where the DecodeME genetics points. Other integrated models are in the atlas, including Hanson's, the Open Medicine Foundation's and Komaroff and Lipkin's.

Onset after infection is replicated across pathogens and in prospective cohorts. A lasting primed state is inferred: it has not been measured directly.

Post-exertional malaise in this model

Post-exertional malaise corresponds to steps 5, 6 and 7 occurring in sequence. Standing or exertion raises the demand for blood flow beyond what the circulation can deliver, and muscle and brain are briefly under-perfused. The resulting injury provokes an immune response that develops over one to three days, which would account for the 12 to 72 hour delay. Immune signals then act on the brain to produce the sickness response, the same pathway responsible for malaise in acute infection. Each episode adds arousal and disturbed sleep (step 8), which would lower the threshold for the next. Many patients report this narrowing threshold, though it has not been measured objectively.

The evidence for this sequence is thin. Muscle biopsies a day after exercise in long COVID show fibre damage, immune-cell infiltration and amyloid deposits (Appelman 2024). In ME/CFS, one study found exaggerated innate immune responses 24 hours after exercise (Che 2025), but a person-by-person re-analysis of the largest two-day exercise proteome (Germain 2025) found no protein whose response differed from controls (atlas). Both sampled blood, and neither sampled at 48 to 72 hours, when symptoms usually peak. That gap is why proposed experiment 1 comes first.

Crashes without physical effort. Severe patients crash after noise, conversation or startle. Sensory load barely raises demand for blood, so this is the observation the model fits worst. Two routes are possible. Arousal may make patients over-breathe, and the fall in CO₂ narrows brain vessels; over-breathing on standing is already documented, so this would put sensory crashes on the same circulatory pathway. Or the sickness response itself may be sensitised and fire at a lower input. A short test with capnography and transcranial Doppler during a seated sensory challenge would separate the two (open question).

Variants worth testing

  • Structural small-vessel disease. If capillaries are lost or their pericytes damaged, blood flow could not rise on demand even with blood volume restored. This would explain why saline and volume expansion help many patients without resolving the illness. Testable with muscle biopsy, OCT angiography and nailfold capillaroscopy.
  • Herpesvirus reactivation driving complement. In long COVID, complement activation tracked antibodies to herpesviruses; complement injures endothelium and activates platelets. Many cases of ME/CFS begin with EBV or CMV. A negative blood PCR does not exclude this. Testable with sC5b-9 and repeat PCR during a crash.
  • Small-fibre neuropathy behind venous pooling. If the nerves that constrict veins are lost, volume support alone would not restore venous return. The controlled NIH study did not find excess small-fibre loss, so this is likely a subgroup at most. Testable with a skin punch biopsy.
  • A persistent immune set point. Interferon-alpha therapy shows that a brief immune exposure can leave lasting fatigue with normal routine blood tests. The normal CRP and ESR seen in most patients fit this variant. If it is right, the sickness response (step 7) is self-sustaining and the circulatory steps are secondary.

Implications for research and treatment

The proposed experiments follow from the weakest parts of the model: the arrows from 5 to 6 and from 6 to 7, which no study in classic ME/CFS has tested; whether steps 3 to 5 set the crash threshold in the same patients; and whether step 2 includes a testable endocrine defect. In short: sample the 72 hours after exertion, measure blood volume, endothelial function, capillaries, breathing and brain blood flow in the same people, look for complement activation in classic ME/CFS as has been done in long COVID, and include severe patients by sampling at home.

The model makes one prediction about treatment that can be checked against trials as they report: treatments acting on steps 3 to 5 should raise the crash threshold, while treatments acting only on step 7 should ease symptoms without changing it.
